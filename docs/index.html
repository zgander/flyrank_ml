Can ML prioritize pages for content refresh review?

A retrospective evaluation of a model that ranks pages for human review using prior search and engagement signals—not a claim that refreshing a page will recover clicks.

0. Abstract

Can observable page-performance signals help prioritize content pages for a human refresh review? This analysis uses the FlyRank ML Internship warehouse, aggregating February 2026 Google Search Console (GSC) and available Google Analytics 4 (GA4) measurements and defining a March 2026 zero-click outcome for eligible pages. A Random Forest classifier was evaluated using a client-grouped holdout, with a random-row split reported only as a diagnostic comparison and a transparent W04 rule as the baseline. On held-out clients, the Random Forest achieved Precision@50 of 0.760, Average Precision of 0.473, and ROC-AUC of 0.853, versus 0.340 Precision@50 for the W04 baseline reported on the same W05 test cohort; the grouped test positive rate was 15.3%. The resulting rank is intended to support human review of pages, not to prove that a refresh will recover clicks or to predict a search engine's ranking algorithm.

1. Problem and decision

The practical question is which pages an editor or SEO analyst should inspect first for a possible content refresh. The model outputs a ranked queue; a human should then inspect intent match, accuracy, indexability, technical health, and business context before deciding whether to make changes. A false positive costs review time, while a false negative may leave a potentially useful page unreviewed.

Target definition. went_dark = 1 when a page has at least one measured GSC day in March 2026 and zero measured GSC clicks that month. This is a measured outcome proxy, not proof of content decay or refresh success.

2. Data and eligibility

The analysis aggregates the FlyRank internship warehouse's fact_content_daily_performance for February and March 2026. February signals are predictors; March clicks define the outcome. The eligible frame contains 46,152 rows across 31 clients and requires at least 500 February GSC impressions plus measured GSC coverage in both months. The overall eligible positive rate was 15.50% before splitting.

Features: February impressions, clicks, CTR, average position, GSC available days, GA4 sessions, and GA4 available days. Client and content IDs are not predictors; client ID is used only for grouping the split. March outcome fields, trend fields, product scores, and action flags are excluded from the feature matrix.

Coverage caveat: requiring measured March GSC data conditions the evaluated population on outcome-window availability. Findings do not automatically apply to pages without that coverage. No client names, domains, URLs, raw search queries, credentials, or row-level queue are published here.

3. Validation design

The primary evaluation uses GroupShuffleSplit(test_size=0.20, random_state=42), ensuring pages from a client do not appear in both train and test. This tests performance on held-out clients. A random-row split is included as a diagnostic only because pages from the same client may occur in both partitions. The metrics are Precision@50, Average Precision (AP), ROC-AUC, and test positive rate.

4. Methodology

All six executable W06 checks passed: February-only predictors; no future/target/product fields in the feature list; IDs excluded from features; zero target-label mismatches; zero client overlap; and all eligible rows above the 500-impression floor. These checks reduce common leakage risks but cannot remove population-selection bias or make the outcome causal.

6. Interpretation and limitations

In the W05 Random Forest, impurity-based feature importance was concentrated in February clicks (0.423), impressions (0.266), and CTR (0.195). Other predictors contributed less, and GA4 sessions had slightly negative mean AP contribution in the permutation-importance run. This suggests the ranking mainly uses prior search-performance scale and click-through signals. Importance is model-specific and does not show that any feature causes future clicks.

  • The target is zero measured March clicks, not a verified need to refresh.
  • The 500-impression threshold and two-month coverage requirement limit generalization.
  • Only one feature month and one outcome month are evaluated; seasonality and longer trends are not tested.
  • The eligible frame has 31 clients, and the W05 grouped test partition has 7 clients.
  • GA4 coverage is sparse; availability flags indicate measurement coverage.
  • Retrospective ranking does not measure refresh impact. A prospective experiment or credible causal design is needed for causal claims.

7. Ranked recommendations

  1. Use the queue for human review, not automatic refreshes. Verify technical health, intent match, content accuracy, and context first.
  2. Review high-visibility pages first. The 500-impression floor is a workflow choice, not a universal or optimized threshold.
  3. Show evidence beside every rank. Display February clicks, impressions, CTR, position, and coverage so reviewers can understand the signal.
  4. Route missing-coverage pages to measurement checks. Do not silently treat missing data as good or bad performance.
  5. Track outcomes prospectively. Log reviewer decisions and subsequent outcomes; where feasible, compare refreshed pages with a credible control group.
  6. Repeat validation across time and clients. Test later feature/outcome windows before operational deployment.

Confidence: moderate that the ranking enriches the top-50 review queue for this retrospective label in the measured sample; low that the ranking identifies pages whose refresh will improve performance.
